The Geometry of Grokking:
Norm Minimization on the Zero-Loss Manifold

Tiberiu Musat
ETH Zurich
tiberiu@musat.ai
Abstract

Grokking is a puzzling phenomenon in neural networks where full generalization occurs only after a substantial delay following the complete memorization of the training data. Previous research has linked this delayed generalization to representation learning driven by weight decay, but the precise underlying dynamics remain elusive. In this paper, we argue that post-memorization learning can be understood through the lens of constrained optimization: gradient descent effectively minimizes the weight norm on the zero-loss manifold. We formally prove this in the limit of infinitesimally small learning rates and weight decay coefficients. To further dissect this regime, we introduce an approximation that decouples the learning dynamics of a subset of parameters from the rest of the network. Applying this framework, we derive a closed-form expression for the post-memorization dynamics of the first layer in a two-layer network. Experiments confirm that simulating the training process using our predicted gradients reproduces both the delayed generalization and representation learning characteristic of grokking.

1 Introduction

Neural networks have achieved great success, but their mechanisms remain far from being fully understood. Doshi-Velez and Kim (2017) argue that understanding the inner workings of neural networks is crucial for the development of AI systems with increased safety and reliability. Moreover, understanding the learning dynamics of neural networks could also help us improve their performance and efficiency: it is easier to design better learning algorithms when we understand the limitations of the existing ones. Furthermore, insights into artificial neural networks may also enhance our understanding of biological neural networks due to their fundamental similarities (Sucholutsky et al., 2023; Kohoutová et al., 2020).

This work aims to clarify the learning dynamics underlying a particularly puzzling phenomenon termed grokking. Under specific training conditions, neural networks achieve generalization on the test data only after an extended period following the complete memorization of the training data. This behavior was first observed in synthetic problems such as modular addition (Power et al., 2022), but was later shown to also happen in real-world datasets (Liu et al., 2022b; Humayun et al., 2024).

In the specific problem of modular addition, interpretability research has revealed that neural networks achieve generalization by placing the embedding vectors on a circle (Gromov, 2023; Zhong et al., 2024). The circular structure of the embedding layer enables the network to perform a symmetric algorithm that generalizes perfectly to unseen data. It is currently known that circular representations emerge gradually during the post-memorization phase (Nanda et al., 2023) and that weight decay is mainly responsible for driving the delayed generalization (Liu et al., 2022b), but the precise dynamics remain unclear.

Moreover, the role of the embedding layer in the modular addition task is a striking example that generalization in neural networks often hinges on representation learning within specific network components. In such cases, it can be highly beneficial to simplify the complex learning dynamics of a deep network by isolating and analyzing only the component of interest. Recent work has already begun to explore such approximations, also referred to as effective theories (van Rossem and Saxe, 2024; Liu et al., 2022a; Musat, 2024; Mehta et al., ).

2 Our Contributions

In this work, we aim to answer the following questions:

  1. Q1.

    What is the exact role of weight decay in the post-memorization learning dynamics?

  2. Q2.

    Can we isolate the dynamics of the embedding layer from the rest of the network?

We answer Q1 in Section 4 by proving that, after memorization is achieved, the learning dynamics approximately follow the minimization of the weight norm, constrained to the zero-loss level set.

We answer Q2 in Section 5 by proposing an approximation for the isolated learning dynamics of any parameter subset as the minimization of a specific cost function.

We then combine these insights in Section 6 to study the post-memorization learning dynamics of a two-layer network, deriving a closed-form expression for the cost function of the first layer (the embedding layer).

Finally, in Section 7, we validate our theoretical insights on a modular addition task, showing that our approximations reproduce the delayed generalization and circular representations characteristic of grokking.

Concurrent Work.

Boursier et al. (2026) offer a very similar answer to Q1 by studying regularized gradient flow in the limit of vanishing weight decay. They formally prove that training decomposes into two distinct phases: a fast initial stage where the network converges to the manifold of critical points, and a slow subsequent stage where the parameters drift along this manifold following a Riemannian gradient flow that decreases the ℓ2\ell_{2}-norm of the weights.

3 Intuitions from Toy Models

Before presenting our theoretical findings, we give an intuition for our results by discussing and visualizing how they relate to a few highly simplified models.

3.1 Grokking Addition

We begin by discussing how a linear model can grok addition from just 1+1=21+1=2. We use a single-layer linear model with two inputs and two weights: y^=w1​x1+w2​x2\hat{y}=w_{1}x_{1}+w_{2}x_{2}. We train this model with mean-squared error loss using just one sample: x1=x2=1,y=2x_{1}=x_{2}=1,\ y=2. We use three different values of weight decay, λ∈{ 0.01,0.1,0.2}\lambda\in\{\,0.01,0.1,0.2\,\}. We initialize our model with w1=−1w_{1}=-1 and w2=1w_{2}=1.

We aim to show that our model can learn to perform standard addition, despite being trained with a single sample. Test accuracy is measured on a set of 100 randomly generated samples, where x1x_{1} and x2x_{2} are sampled from a normal distribution, and y=x1+x2y=x_{1}+x_{2}.

We show our results in Figure 1. We can see that our model reproduces grokking: training loss becomes very low after just a 10 steps, while test loss takes a few hundred steps. Additionally, the model achieves lower loss with smaller λ\lambda, but takes longer to generalize.

Interpretation.

From Figure 1 (a, b), we can see that learning follows two phases. In the first phase, driven by loss minimization, the model achieves a low loss by learning (w1,w2)≈(0,2)(w_{1},w_{2})\approx(0,2). In the second phase, learning is entirely driven by weight decay. The model follows norm minimization, while maintaining (almost) zero loss, eventually reaching (w1,w2)≈(1,1)(w_{1},w_{2})\approx(1,1). Note that, for smaller λ\lambda, the model remains closer to the zero-loss line, but generalization also takes longer.

3.2 Norm Minimization

While the previous example illustrates our theoretical framework, it does not capture its full generality. It is unsurprising that applying weight decay encourages a reduction in norm. However, the central claim of this paper is significantly stronger: we argue that the learning dynamics under weight decay do not merely follow some norm-decreasing direction, but rather evolve along the direction that maximally decreases the norm, subject to remaining on the zero-loss manifold. Another way to view this is the following: once the model achieves perfect memorization, learning effectively follows gradient descent on the weight norm, constrained to the zero-loss manifold.

To offer a better intuition, we show how a linear model can grok three-number addition. We train a three-parameter linear model y^=w1​x1+w2​x2+w3​x3\hat{y}\,=\,w_{1}x_{1}+w_{2}x_{2}+w_{3}x_{3} with just one sample: x1=x2=x3=1,y=3x_{1}=x_{2}=x_{3}=1,\ y=3. As in the previous section, this model exhibits grokking: after quickly achieving zero training loss, the model slowly reaches the generalizing solution (w1,w2,w3)≈(1,1,1)(w_{1},w_{2},w_{3})\approx(1,1,1). We perform four training runs with different initializations and visualize the resulting trajectories in Figure 2. We observe that, for all initializations, the model first converges to the zero-loss plane, then moves directly towards the solution of minimum norm.

3.3 A Few Mathematical Nuances

So far, our examples have shown only flat zero-loss subspaces, but this is not necessarily the case. The zero-loss subspace can more generally be thought of as a manifold: a subspace that locally resembles Euclidean space near each point. For example, we show a curved zero-loss set in Figure 3 (left), along with a few training trajectories.

An important caveat is that the zero-loss set is not necessarily a manifold everywhere: it might contain singular points. Such a singular point is demonstrated in Figure 3 (center). However, such singularities should not worry us too much. As we prove in Theorem 4.10, if the network is realized by a smooth function, we will almost never encounter a singularity during standard training.

Another nuance is that, in practice, neural networks are trained using the ReLU activation function, which is not smooth. This will partition the loss into a finite set of smooth regions with nonsmooth boundaries. The nonsmooth points will also form a null set. We visualize a scenario of this type in Figure 3 (right) with leaky ReLU activation: ReLU​(x)=x​if​x>0​else​x/10.\mathrm{ReLU}(x)=x\mathrm{\;if\;}x>0\mathrm{\;else\;}{x/10}.

We further illustrate our key theoretical result using a toy loss landscape. Consider a model with only two parameters x,y∈ℝx,y\in\mathbb{R} and a loss function ℒ​(x,y)=(y−x2)2\mathcal{L}(x,y)=(y-x^{2})^{2}. We visualize this loss landscape in Figure 4. 4.1 Architecture

We consider a neural network trained with mean-squared error loss on kk samples and weight decay:

4.2 Theoretical Setup

We theoretically study the training dynamics under the following assumptions:

Assumption 4.1 (Over-Parametrization).

We assume that d≥k​md\geq km in order for the model to be able to memorize the entire dataset without learning any representations.

Assumption 4.2 (Smooth Network).

We assume that the network realization function ff is continuously differentiable d−k​m+1d-km+1 times.

Assumption 4.3 (Gradient Flow).

We model the gradient descent trajectory as a gradient flow. We consider the parameter vector as a continuous function of time θ:ℝ≥0→ℝd\theta:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d} with dynamics:

∂θ​(t)∂t=−∇ℒλ​(θ​(t)).\frac{\partial\theta(t)}{\partial t}=-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(\theta(t)). (3)
Assumption 4.4 (Perfect Memorization).

We study the training dynamics after perfect memorization is achieved. This requires that the zero-loss set is not empty, i.e. 𝒵≠∅\mathcal{Z}\neq\varnothing.

Assumption 4.5 (Vanishing Weight Decay).

We study the learning dynamics in the approximation of a very small weight decay coefficient, i.e. λ→0\lambda\rightarrow 0, motivated by the small values of λ\lambda typically used by practitioners (Smith, 2018), as well as by previous empirical works on grokking (Liu et al., 2022b) suggesting that a small λ\lambda is essential for the delayed-generalization phenomona.

4.3 Constrained to the Zero-Loss Set

We begin by establishing that the model remains constrained arbitrarily close to the zero-loss set after reaching a memorizing solution.

Definition 4.6 (Zero-Loss Set).

Let 𝒵={θ∈ℝd|ℒ​(θ)=0}\mathcal{Z}=\{\,\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,|\,\mathcal{L}(\theta)=0\,\} denote the zero-loss set.

Definition 4.7 (Distance).

Let dist𝒵​(θ)=infϕ∈𝒵‖θ−ϕ‖\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{Z}}(\theta)=\inf_{\phi\in\mathcal{Z}}\|\theta-\phi\| be the distance from θ∈ℝd\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d} to 𝒵\mathcal{Z}.

Theorem 4.8 (Stability of 𝒵\mathcal{Z}).

For every trajectory starting at a zero-loss solution θ​(0)∈𝒵\theta(0)\in\mathcal{Z} and every ε>0\varepsilon>0, there exists λε>0\lambda_{\varepsilon}>0 such that for all 0<λ<λε0<\lambda<\lambda_{\varepsilon} the trajectory under ℒλ\mathcal{L}_{\lambda} satisfies

supt≥0dist𝒵​(θ​(t))<ε.\sup_{t\geq 0}\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{Z}}(\theta(t))<\varepsilon. (4)
Sketch of the Proof.

Our proof is based on the fact that the gradient flow will never increase the optimized quantity ℒλ​(θ)=ℒ​(θ)+λ​‖θ‖2\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(\theta)=\mathcal{L}(\theta)+\lambda\|\theta\|^{2}. Since both terms are non-negative, we can establish any desired bound on ℒ​(θ)\mathcal{L}(\theta) by an appropriate choice of λ\lambda. We then use this to obtain the bound on the distance. We give the full proof in Appendix A. ∎

4.4 Regularity of the Zero-Loss Set

We show that the zero-loss set is well-behaved for almost every dataset.

Definition 4.9 (Singular Points).

We say that θ∈ℝd\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d} is a singular point if the Jacobian matrix of ℱ\mathcal{F} at θ\theta is not full rank, i.e. rank​(∇ℱ​(θ))<min⁡(d,k​m)\mathrm{rank}(\nabla\mathcal{F}(\theta))<\min(d,km). We denote the set of all singular points as Crit​(ℱ)⊂ℝk​m\mathrm{Crit}(\mathcal{F})\subset\mathbb{R}^{km}. Note that singular points are defined in terms of ℱ\mathcal{F}, though they correspond to singular points of 𝒵\mathcal{Z}. With respect to ℱ\mathcal{F}, they are more precisely critical points.

Theorem 4.10 (Regularity of 𝒵\mathcal{Z}).

For almost every dataset (xi,yi)i≤k(x_{i},y_{i})_{i\leq k}, the corresponding zero-loss set 𝒵\mathcal{Z} will not contain any singular points.

Sketch of the Proof.

We show that for any set of input vectors (xi)i≤k(x_{i})_{i\leq k}, almost every possible set of target vectors (yi)i≤k(y_{i})_{i\leq k} induces a zero-loss set 𝒵\mathcal{Z} that is completely free of singular points.

Using the assumption that ℱ\mathcal{F} is smooth, our desired result follows almost immediately from Sard (1942), also known as the Morse–Sard theorem, which states that the image of the critical points has Lebesgue measure zero. In our case, the image of the singular points ℱ​(Crit​(ℱ))\mathcal{F}\big(\mathrm{Crit}(\mathcal{F})\big) has Lebesgue measure zero in the space of possible outputs ℝk​m\mathbb{R}^{km}. In other words, only a negligible set of possible outputs is ever hit by a singular point. We give a detailed proof in Appendix B. ∎

Remark 4.15.

In other words, the loss gradient does not induce any movement near 𝒵\mathcal{Z}. After a memorizing solution is reached, learning will be driven entirely by weight decay. The loss will only serve to keep the model near 𝒵\mathcal{Z}, while weight decay will be free to push the model towards norm minimization along any of the tangent directions.

5 Isolated Dynamics of a Network Component

The parameter vector can be decomposed into two orthogonal parameter subsets θ=[θ1,θ2]\theta=[\theta_{1},\theta_{2}], where θ1∈ℝd1\theta_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}, θ2∈ℝd2\theta_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}, and d1+d2=dd_{1}+d_{2}=d. We are interested in the learning dynamics of θ1\theta_{1}:

θ˙1=−∇θ1ℒλ​(θ1,θ2)\dot{\theta}_{1}=-\nabla_{\theta_{1}}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}) (6)

The gradient flow assumption means that the trajectory of θ1\theta_{1} is a one-dimensional curve in a d1d_{1}-dimensional space. This suggests that it is highly unlikely that our trajectory will pass through the same θ1\theta_{1} twice. If we assume that a training trajectory only goes through unique values of θ1\theta_{1}, then it is possible to parametrize θ2\theta_{2} as a function of θ1\theta_{1}:

θ2=ϕ​(θ1)\theta_{2}=\phi(\theta_{1}) (7)

where ϕ:ℝd1→ℝd2\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}} is a function specific to the loss function and the initial parameters.

We motivate this assumption based on the comprehensive literature on multiple points of stochastic processes. For example, even with a dimensionality as small as d≥4d\geq 4, it is known that a Brownian motion in ℝd\mathbb{R}^{d} contains no self-intersections almost surely (Mörters and Peres (2010), Chapter 9). More generally, Dalang et al. (2021) prove the non-existance of multiple points for a wide class of Gaussian random fields.

This parametrization allows us to isolate the dynamics of θ1\theta_{1} along the training trajectory by expressing them as a function of θ1\theta_{1} alone:

θ˙1=−∇θ1ℒλ​(θ1,ϕ​(θ1))\dot{\theta}_{1}=-\nabla_{\theta_{1}}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(\theta_{1},\phi(\theta_{1})) (8)

While the function ϕ\phi is generally intractable, working with reasonable approximations can provide valuable insights into the learning dynamics of θ1\theta_{1}.

5.1 Approximate Cost Function

We propose approximating ϕ\phi by assuming that parameters θ2\theta_{2} are optimal for the current value of θ1\theta_{1}:

ϕ​(θ1)=arg⁡minθ2⁡ℒλ​(θ1,θ2)\phi(\theta_{1})=\arg\min_{\theta_{2}}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}) (9)

This approximation can also be understood as treating θ1\theta_{1} as the slow learning component, while θ2\theta_{2} is the fast learning component that quickly adapts to the current value of θ1\theta_{1}.

Additionally, optimizing θ\theta under this approximation is equivalent to optimizing the following cost function:

ℛ​(θ1)=minθ2⁡ℒλ​(θ1,θ2)\mathcal{R}(\theta_{1})=\min_{\theta_{2}}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}) (10)

The learning dynamics of θ1\theta_{1} under Equations 8 and 9 follow the gradient flow of ℛ\mathcal{R} when ϕ\phi is differentiable:

Proof.

Note that ℛ​(θ1)=ℒλ​(θ1,ϕ​(θ1))\mathcal{R}(\theta_{1})=\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(\theta_{1},\phi(\theta_{1})). By differentiating it with respect to θ1\theta_{1} we obtain that ∇θ1ℛ​(θ1)=∇θ1ℒλ​(θ1,ϕ​(θ1))+∇θ2ℒλ​(θ1,ϕ​(θ1))​∇θ1ϕ​(θ1)\nabla_{\theta_{1}}\mathcal{R}(\theta_{1})=\nabla_{\theta_{1}}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(\theta_{1},\phi(\theta_{1}))+\nabla_{\theta_{2}}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(\theta_{1},\phi(\theta_{1}))\,\nabla_{\theta_{1}}\phi(\theta_{1}). However, since ϕ​(θ1)\phi(\theta_{1}) is a minimum of ℒλ\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}, we have that ∇θ2ℒλ​(θ1,ϕ​(θ1))=0\nabla_{\theta_{2}}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(\theta_{1},\phi(\theta_{1}))=0, giving us the desired result. ∎

We further assume a strongly overparametrized regime with more hidden units that training samples (dh>nd_{h}>n). This allows the second layer to fit the outputs perfectly almost always. Using a discrete Fourier transform, we decompose the embedding matrix EE into a linear combination of circles with different frequencies:

This suggests that each circle leverages a different subset of hidden units.

8 Conclusion

We have formally established that the learning dynamics of neural networks in the grokking regime approximate as the minimization of the weight norm within the zero-loss set. Additionally, we have established a theoretical basis for approximating the learning dynamics of individual network components.

Limitations.

This work does not cover cross-entropy loss, which is commonly used in practice. With regard to isolated dynamics, our work is limited to the case of two-layer networks. Exciting challenges lie ahead in understanding the grokking dynamics of more complex settings and architectures.

Impact Statement.

We believe that understanding the learning dynamics of neural networks is essential for the design of more efficient and accurate AI systems. However, the development and deployment of such systems should be approached with caution.

Appendix D Experimental Details for Section 4.6 (Similarity of Dynamics)

We empirically validate our theory that post-memorization dynamics follows the norm-minimization direction on the zero-loss manifold. We train a few small networks to perform modular addition using two layer, ReLU activation, mean squared-error loss, and weight decay.

Setup.

We use the same architecture and dataset as in Section 7. We train two-layer networks to perform modular addition with two numbers with a fixed modulo nn. The network is given two numbers a,b∈{1,…,N}a,b\in\{1,\ldots,N\} and must output their sum. The network has nn input neurons, hh hidden neurons, and nn output neurons. ReLU activation is applied on the hidden layer. The input is the sum of the one-hot vector representations of aa and bb. The target output is the one-hot vector representation of a+bmodna+b\mod n. In order to accelerate the calculation of the Jacobian matrix, we use very small networks with n=11n=11 and h=128h=128. There are n​(n+1)/2=77n(n+1)/2=77 pairs of numbers in the data set.
